PAGE NOTES: Topic 3 — Electrons & Qubits

- An electron's spin acts like a tiny magnet that can be measured UP |0>
  or DOWN |1>. A qubit is any such two-state quantum system.
- Before measurement it can be in a combination:
  cos(theta/2)|0> + sin(theta/2)|1>. The amplitudes are cos(theta/2) and
  sin(theta/2); their SQUARES are the probabilities. P(UP) = cos^2(theta/2).
- The slider sets P(UP). The arrow's tilt is theta = 2 arccos(sqrt(P_up)):
  0 deg = certainly UP, 90 deg = 50/50, 180 deg = certainly DOWN. This page
  keeps the relative phase at zero.
- One measurement gives UP or DOWN at random and the state snaps to that
  result. Measuring the SAME qubit again right away gives the same result
  (the "Measure the same qubit again" button shows this). "Measure 1x"
  prepares a fresh qubit each time.
- Out of 100 measurements at probability p, expect about 100p UP, give or
  take about sqrt(100 p (1-p)) (5 for p = 0.5; 4.6 for p = 0.7). Exactly
  100p is not guaranteed. More measurements bring the FRACTION closer to p.
- At 100% UP or 100% DOWN there is no randomness.
- What this page leaves out: the relative phase between UP and DOWN. It is
  what distinguishes a superposition from a classical mixture ("really UP
  or DOWN, we just don't know which"). Point curious students to "Go
  deeper: Superposition vs. mixture".
- Compared with a coin: a spinning coin also gives 50/50 results, but it
  has no phase and can't be set to interfere; a resting coin is just a
  classical bit.
