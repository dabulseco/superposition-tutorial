PAGE NOTES: Topic 1 — Bits & Superposition

- A classical bit (the light switch, or a computer voltage of 0 V or 5 V) is
  in exactly one of two states. Measuring it reports that state every time
  and changes nothing. Measured 10 times, an ON switch reads ON 10 times.
- The spinning coin is the page's first picture of a superposition: while
  it spins you can't say heads or tails; slapping it down ("measuring")
  gives exactly one of them.
- One slap can't be predicted. Many slaps can: about half are heads. For
  100 coins the count is usually between 40 and 60 (the typical spread is
  about 5, from sqrt(100 x 0.5 x 0.5)). Exactly 50 is the single most
  likely count, but it still happens only about 8% of the time, so "close
  to 50, rarely exactly 50" is the right expectation. More coins bring the
  FRACTION closer to 50%.
- The page's expandable note "Where the analogy breaks" matters: the coin's
  outcome is fixed by ordinary physics we can't track, so it is really a
  classical mixture. Encourage students to open "Go deeper: Superposition
  vs. mixture" to see an experiment that tells a superposition from a
  mixture.
- A coin RESTING on the table is a classical bit, not a superposition.
