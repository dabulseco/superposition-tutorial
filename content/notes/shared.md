SHARED NOTES (true on every page of the primer)

The one story across the topics
- A classical bit is in exactly one of two states.
- A superposition is a combination of two basic states at once. Each part
  has an amplitude (a weight). A relative phase between the parts also
  matters.
- The same picture describes three systems in this primer:
  - Coupled oscillators: Pattern 1 (weights move in step) and Pattern 2
    (weights move opposite). The mix is set by mode amplitudes A1 and A2.
  - Qubit: UP |0> and DOWN |1>. The squares of the amplitudes are the
    measurement probabilities, and there is a relative phase.
  - Phi-bit: a left-traveling wave (State 0) and a right-traveling wave
    (State 1) on a chain of weights. The mix is set by the wave amplitudes
    a_L and a_R and a relative phase.
- Each of these can be drawn as two amplitude bars and as a point on a
  sphere (the "state card" shown on the pages).

Words to use carefully
- "Mixed state" is the technical term for a CLASSICAL MIXTURE: each system
  is definitely in one state or the other and we just don't know which.
  That is the opposite of a superposition. For a superposition say "a
  combination of the two", never "a mixed state" or "a mix".
- Amplitude and power are different. Power (and probability) goes as the
  amplitude squared. On the phi-bit page the sliders set amplitudes; the
  "share of State 1" is a_R^2 / (a_L^2 + a_R^2), the same formula as a
  qubit's probability.

Where the spinning-coin analogy helps and where it breaks
- It helps: before you look, you can't say heads or tails; looking gives
  exactly one of them; many trials give about 50/50.
- It breaks: a spinning coin's result is decided by ordinary physics we
  just can't follow. That makes it closer to a classical mixture. It has
  no relative phase and can't show interference. A real superposition is
  different in a way an experiment can show: see the page "Go deeper:
  Superposition vs. mixture".
- A coin RESTING on a table is simply a classical bit in a definite state.

Classical analogs and what is different about a qubit
- Oscillator patterns and phi-bit waves are classical. Both parts of the
  combination can be observed at once, and looking doesn't change them.
- Measuring a qubit gives only UP or DOWN, at random with the set
  probabilities, and the state snaps to the result. Measuring again right
  away gives the same result.
